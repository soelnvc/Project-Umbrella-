RakshaWall: a privacy firewall that attacks its own release before it ships

Track 3 · Secret Wards · Team: JenSON · Members: Siddhesh Goel (leader), Krishna Bhatia, Nithin Jagganatham · Repo: github.com/soelnvc/Project-Umbrella-
RakshaWall turns multilingual Indian hospital records (English, Hindi, Hinglish; CSV, JSON, FHIR, free text) into a research-ready release that is offline, CPU-only and model-free. It links each patient across files, hospitals and scripts under one keyed fake ID and gives that patient one consistent treatment everywhere. Every output file is then re-read by a release gate, and the release is measured against a copy of the published attacker.

1. Architecture

2. How each part works

  • Ingestion. Format and encoding are read from the content, not the extension (JSON behind .csv, UTF-16, BOMs). JSON of any depth is parsed without recursion. Empty, truncated, binary, PDF and XML files are quarantined with a reason code; nothing broken is copied out.
  • Detection (3 scripts). Patterns (Aadhaar, ABHA, PAN, phones with digit checks, emails, record numbers by shape), a column profiler that classifies fields by their values with header hints in English, Hindi and Hinglish, and context rules for names: relation markers (S/o, पुत्र), honorifics, labels, postpositions, chat senders and attendants. Text is also read through a normalised view (HTML entities, %-encoding, zero-width characters, look-alike letters) and inside base64 and hex payloads, file names, JSON keys and headers. A clinical shield protects doses, frequencies, durations, doctors, wards, eponyms (Bell's palsy), C/O complaints versus care-of names, and ASHA the job versus Asha the name.
  • Linkage & pseudonyms. Our own Devanagari-to-Latin transliteration and a phonetic skeleton make राम कुमार, Ram Kumaar and RAMKUMAR comparable. Records link through strong IDs, or through a name plus a second clue (relative, DOB, village and age); gender, DOB, unique-ID and relative conflicts forbid a merge, and a one-word name joins only a unique full-name group. Fake IDs are HMAC-SHA256 over the patient's anchor record with a subkey of RAKSHA_SECRET, placed in the patient's record-number fields.
  • Privacy layer. Per patient: one secret date shift of 30-180 days (intervals and stays stay exact), one age band holding every age seen (10-year, 20-year for small groups, 90+), village to tehsil, 3-digit PIN. Dates of birth are withheld (FHIR: the standard data-absent-reason "masked" marker). Look-alike groups below k=5 move to district and wider bands. Diagnoses held by 3 or fewer patients become their ICD-10 chapter, rare procedures are withheld, and high-risk discharges move one extra day. Security is in section 5.

3. USP

  • It attacks itself. A release gate re-reads every output the way the organizers do (normalised, base64 decoded, digits across separators) and refuses it if any of the file's own identifiers survive. Our copy of the published attacker drove the policy: it showed a shifted DOB keeps the birth year and re-identified 69% of patients, so DOBs are now withheld (15%).
  • One patient, one treatment, across scripts. Cross-script linking with no wrong merges, and the same shift, band and place in every file, so pooling a patient's records gives an attacker nothing new.
  • Meaning, not word lists. Context rules separate a doctor from a relative with the same first name, a complaint from a care-of name and a disease eponym from a person: 96.6% of medical values survive.

What we give up and why. A shifted date of birth keeps the birth year, which pins the age: withholding DOBs cut pooled re-identification from 69% to 15%, at the cost of the DOB field's utility credit; 10-year bands cost a little precision. Tehsil instead of village and 3-digit PINs keep regional analysis, and per-patient shifts keep every interval and stay length exact (high-risk discharges move one day). The only medical values lost (3.4%) are rare diagnoses turned into their ICD-10 chapter and rare procedures, which make a patient unique on their own. Common diagnoses are kept, so the targeted attack still succeeds against 57%; hiding them would cost far more clinical value than it protects. In a release of 57 patients nearly everyone is unique on the four attributes above, so the realistic measure is the attacker's, who must find the person in a register 4-5 times larger.

5. Security design

  • Assume the output is breached. It holds no direct identifiers, no birth dates, no raw record numbers and no original file names (outputs are named doc_<HMAC>). There is deliberately no re-identification vault: nothing reversible exists to steal.
  • Keys. RAKSHA_SECRET lives only in memory; separate HMAC subkeys are derived for fake IDs, date shifts, ID tokens and file names. Without it, fake IDs cannot be inverted (unkeyed hashes, encodings and digit cores are tested) and two releases cannot be linked.
  • Logs and errors hold event codes and counts only; exceptions are reported by class name. No values, file names or secrets reach logs, the report, temp files or the home folder (checked by tools/verify.sh).
  • Fail closed. Each file runs in its own error boundary; a failure quarantines that file, never releases it. A watchdog keeps the run inside the time budget and the report always matches what is on disk.

6. Known limitations

  • Rules, not models. A name with no context, in no word list and in a format unlike anything seen could be missed. The scrambled tests and the gate reduce this risk, they do not remove it.
  • Targeted attack (57%) remains high because common diagnoses are kept; our attacker is also weaker than the official one on record linkage and the targeted attack, so those two numbers may understate the real risk.
  • k=5 cannot be reached on data this small even at district and 20-year bands; rare patients are protected by the rare-value rules instead.
  • PDF, image and XML files are quarantined, not cleaned. A single 25 MB JSON export runs in 104 s but needs 1.6 GB of memory. Ambiguous dates (03/04) rely on the file's own dates or its source's convention.
  • Next: context-scored diagnosis generalisation for the targeted attack, and streaming very large files.

Everything above is reproducible: tools/verify.sh --submit --scale runs the tests, determinism and different-secret checks, the organizers' checks, the scrambled-data leak check, our attacker and the scaling test.
