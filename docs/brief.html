Umbrella: a privacy firewall that attacks its own release before it ships

Track 3 · Secret Wards · Team: JenSON · Members: Siddhesh Goel (leader), Krishna Bhatia, Nithin Jagganatham · Repo: github.com/soelnvc/Project-Umbrella-
Umbrella turns multilingual Indian hospital exports (English, Hindi, Hinglish; CSV, JSON, FHIR R4, free text) into an offline, CPU-only, model-free research release. It links each patient across files, hospitals and scripts under one keyed pseudonym, applies one consistent privacy plan everywhere, checks every output in memory via a release gate, and audits the release against the published voter-list attacker.

1. Architecture

2. How each part works

  • Ingestion. Format and encoding are sniffed from bytes, not extensions (JSON inside .csv, tables inside .txt, UTF-16, BOMs, strict UTF-8, legacy cp1252). JSON of any depth parses iteratively without recursion. Empty, damaged, binary, PDF and XML files are quarantined with a fixed reason code — never copied raw.
  • Detection (3 scripts). Shape rules (Aadhaar, ABHA, PAN, phones incl. Indic digits, emails, hospital IDs), a value-driven column profiler with English/Hindi/Hinglish hints, and context rules (surname-first, initials, S/o/पुत्र, honorifics, postpositions, attendants, chats). Runs over a normalised view (entities, %XX, \uXXXX, zero-width, diacritics, homoglyphs) and inside base64/hex payloads, keys, headers and paths. A clinical shield keeps doses, durations, doctors, wards, eponyms (Bell's palsy), C/O symptoms vs. persons, and ASHA worker vs. Asha.
  • Linkage & pseudonyms. Custom Devanagari-to-Latin transliteration and phonetic skeletons match मोहम्मद शाहिद, Mohammad Shahid and Mohd. Shahid. Linear compound blocking on name + second clue (DOB, relative, village/age) retains common surnames; gender, DOB, ID and relative conflicts veto merges. Keyed HMAC-SHA256 IDs (PSN-) replace patient record-number fields and FHIR Patient.identifier.
  • Privacy layer. Per patient: one keyed ±30–180d shift (stays and intervals remain exact), withheld DOB (FHIR data-absent-reason: masked), one truthful age band (10/20-yr, 90+), village → tehsil, and 3-digit PIN. Groups below k=5 escalate to district and 20-yr bands; cohort-scaled rare diagnoses (≤1 to ≤3 patients) become ICD-10 chapters, rare procedures are withheld, and high-risk discharges jitter +1 day.

3. USP

  • It attacks itself before commit. An in-memory release gate re-reads every output with scorer normalisation, base64/hex decoding and cross-separator digit checks, refusing leaks. Our copy of the published attacker drove policy: shifted DOBs kept birth years and let pooled attacks re-identify 69% of patients, so DOBs are withheld (cutting risk to 23%).
  • One patient, one treatment, across scripts. Cross-script linking with zero wrong merges applies the exact same shift, age band and geography in every file, so pooling a patient's longitudinal records yields no narrowing intersection.
  • Clinical meaning, not naive word lists. Context disambiguates a doctor from a relative sharing a first name, a symptom complaint from a care-of relative, and clinical eponyms from people — preserving 99.2% of medical values.

Why we chose these trade-offs
  • Withheld DOB + 10/20-year age bands: Shifting DOB still reveals birth year; masking DOB slashed pooled re-identification from 69% → 23% while truthful bands preserve age-stratified epidemiology.
  • Tehsil + 3-digit PIN + per-patient date shifts: Preserves regional public-health clustering and keeps every follow-up interval and stay length exact (high-risk discharges move +1 day to defeat exact-stay matching).
  • Cohort-scaled rare conditions vs. common diagnoses: Only rare diagnoses (mapped to ICD-10 chapters) and rare procedures cost the remaining 0.8% of medical values. Common diagnoses stay intact, explaining the 58% targeted-attack ceiling (suppressing common conditions would destroy clinical utility). In a 57-patient cohort 50/57 look unique internally, whereas the real attacker must search a voter register 4–5× larger.

5. Security design

  • Assume breach — zero reversible state at rest. Released files hold no direct PII, birth dates, raw MRNs or PII paths (renamed to doc_<HMAC> / dir_<HMAC>). Because we store no re-identification vault or lookup table on disk, a breached output server has nothing reversible to steal or decrypt.
  • In-memory key separation. RAKSHA_SECRET stays exclusively in RAM and derives isolated HMAC-SHA256 subkeys for pseudonyms, date shifts, ID tokens and path hashes — defeating rainbow tables, digit-core leaks and cross-release linkage.
  • Zero-PII logs and side channels. Logs emit event codes and integer counts only; exceptions report class names only. input/ is read-only and TMPDIR, HOME, stdout/stderr and report.json stay PII-free.
  • Fail-closed quarantine & watchdog. Per-file error boundaries quarantine failures into ledger-tracked reasons outside sanitized/; a 720 s watchdog guarantees clean completion and disk-to-ledger reconciliation.

6. Known limitations

  • Deterministic rules, zero models: An unlabelled name absent from all context cues and lexicons in an unseen layout could be missed (mitigated by value-based column profiling, chaos testing and the release gate).
  • Targeted attack (58%): Remains elevated because common diagnoses are kept; our local attacker is also simpler than the official evaluator on linkage and targeted attacks, so those two figures may understate official risk.
  • Small-cohort k=5: Unreachable on 57 patients even at district + 20-year bands; rare-value rules protect outliers instead.
  • Binary formats & single giant JSONs: PDF, image and XML files are quarantined rather than OCR/parsed; a single 25 MB JSON export finishes in 104 s at 1.6 GB RSS; ambiguous dates (03/04) rely on file/source conventions.
  • Next: Context-scored diagnosis generalisation against targeted attacks, and streaming linkage for multi-GB JSONs.
